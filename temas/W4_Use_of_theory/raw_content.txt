A theory is a set of constructs (variables), definitions, and interrelated propositions that presents a systematic view of phenomena by specifying the relationships among variables, with the aim of explaining natural phenomena.

Procedure: to study interrelated variables, whose relationships take the form of propositions or hypotheses.

W. Lawrence Neuman(2004)
Macro-level theory: Social theory that focuses on the macro level of social life (e.g., social institutions, major sectors of society, entire societies, or world regions) and on processes occurring over long periods (many years, multiple decades, or a century or more).

Meso-level theory: Social theory that concentrates on relationships, processes, and structures at an intermediate level of social life (e.g., organisations, movements, communities), with events unfolding over moderate durations (many months, several years, or a decade).
Micro-level theory: Social theory that examines the micro level of social life, focusing on short-term interactions (e.g., face‑to‑face interactions and encounters among individuals or small groups).

Confounding Variables
When evaluating causal claims, it is crucial to assess the existence of an unmeasured third variable (Z) that may influence the outcome.
In quantitative research, if a confounding variable is not measured, it may limit the ability to establish a causal relationship.

How to Present a Causal Link?
Use of Hypotheses
Introduce the theory by using sets of hypotheses centred on the identified variables.

Main hypothesis: Higher levels of education (X) are associated with higher individual income (Y).

Mediating hypothesis: Parental income (Z) affects an individual’s level of education (X), which in turn influences their income (Y).

Confounding hypothesis: Parental income (Z) directly affects an individual’s income (Y), in addition to the influence of education (X).
Conditional Statements
Provide an explanation of why independent variables are expected to influence or cause dependent variables.

If an individual’s level of education (X) increases, then their income (Y) will also increase.

Individuals with higher educational attainment express their skills and capabilities in professional and economic activities
which may further enhance their income. 
Additionally, higher parental income (Z) may influence the level of education obtained by their children, which subsequently affects their income.
Visual Models
Place dependent variables on the right-hand side of the diagram and independent variables on the left.
Use unidirectional arrows from each determining variable to each variable it influences.
Indicate the strength and direction of relationships by inserting positive (+) or negative (–) valence signs.
Use double‑headed arrows to represent unanalysed relationships between variables that do not depend on other relationships in the model.

How to Include Theory?
In quantitative studies, theory is used deductively and is placed near the beginning of the proposed study. 

The aim is to test or verify a theory: the researcher advances a theory, collects data to test it, and reflects on whether it is confirmed or disconfirmed.

The theory becomes a framework for the entire study, serving as an organising model for the research questions or hypotheses and for the procedures of data collection.

The researcher tests or verifies a theory by examining hypotheses or questions derived from it.

These hypotheses or questions contain variables (or constructs) that must be defined by the researcher.
Identification and review of a theory
Formulation of questions and hypotheses derived from the theory
Definition and operationalisation of variables based on the theory
Measurement of variables, using instruments, to test the hypotheses

A theoretical perspective provides general guidance for the study's questions concerning gender, class, and race (or other issues related to inequalities).

Endpoint or Closing Point
In contrast to the deductive use of theory and the theoretical‑standpoint orientation, qualitative studies may employ theory as an endpoint for the study.
This theoretical endpoint implies that the qualitative study concludes with a theoretical model, a theory, or a conceptual model. 

Qualitative research is an inductive process: it begins with data, moves toward broader themes, and eventually leads to a generalised model or theory.
The process begins by gathering detailed information from participants, which is then organised into categories or themes.

These themes are later developed into broader patterns, theories, or generalisations, which are compared with personal experiences or the existing literature on the topic.

The development of themes and categories into patterns, theories, or generalisations suggests different possible endpoints for qualitative studies.

Unlike the deductive form found in quantitative studies, these pattern‑based theories represent interconnected ideas or components linked to a coherent whole.
Open‑ended questions are developed and information is collected
Secondary data are examined to form themes and categories
Themes and categories are developed into broader patterns, theories, or generalisations
The researcher proposes generalisations or theories
