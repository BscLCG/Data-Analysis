# Week 4: The Use of Theory in Research

## Overview

This week explores the critical role that theory plays in shaping research. It examines how theories are identified, defined, and applied differently depending on whether the study employs a quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods design. The materials also discuss how to operationalize variables, establish causal relationships, and construct visual theoretical models.

## Key Concepts

- **Theory:** A set of interrelated constructs, definitions, and propositions that present a systematic view of a phenomenon to explain or predict outcomes.
- **Levels of Theory:** 
  - *Macro:* Large-scale social institutions over long periods.
  - *Meso:* Intermediate structures like organizations or communities.
  - *Micro:* Short-term, face-to-face interactions.
- **Types of Variables:**
  - *Independent / Predictor:* Variables that influence outcomes.
  - *Dependent / Response:* The outcomes being influenced.
  - *Mediating:* Variables that transmit the effect of an independent variable to a dependent variable.
  - *Moderating:* Variables that affect the strength or direction of a relationship.
  - *Confounding:* Unmeasured variables that may spuriously influence the outcome.
- **Theoretical Visual Models:** Diagrams connecting variables with directional arrows and signs (+/-) to map hypothesized causal relationships.

## Relevant Points

- **Quantitative Use of Theory:** Used deductively to test hypotheses. The theory provides a rigid framework at the beginning of the study to operationalize variables and predict outcomes.
- **Qualitative Use of Theory:** More flexible. It can be used deductively as a broad explanation, as a theoretical standpoint (e.g., critical or feminist theory) to guide data collection, or inductively as the endpoint/conclusion of the study.
- **Causality vs. Correlation:** Establishing causality requires more than statistical correlation; it demands a credible causal identification strategy and a robust research design (such as experiments) to control for confounding variables.

## Detailed Reading 1: The Use of Theory in Qualitative and Mixed Methods Research

In research, a theory is a set of interrelated constructs, definitions, and propositions that present a systematic view of a phenomenon. Its purpose is to explain or predict outcomes. Researchers locate theories at three levels: macro-level theories explain large-scale social institutions over long periods; meso-level theories cover intermediate structures like organizations or communities; and micro-level theories explain short-term, face-to-face interactions.

The application of theory varies fundamentally depending on the chosen research design. In qualitative research, researchers employ theory in three distinct ways.

First, qualitative researchers use theory as a broad explanation for behaviors and attitudes. In this deductive approach, the researcher begins the study with an established theory, collects data to test or confirm the theory, and frames the entire project around it.

Second, qualitative researchers use theory as a theoretical lens or standpoint. This approach relies on paradigms such as critical theory, feminist perspectives, or racialized discourses. The theoretical lens directs the researcher's attention to specific issues, such as power dynamics or social inequalities, and guides the entire research process from data collection to advocacy. This is the foundation of the [transformative worldview](../W1_Intro_and_research/README.md).

Third, qualitative researchers use theory as the endpoint of the study. In this inductive approach, the researcher begins by gathering detailed information from participants. The researcher then forms categories and themes, and finally develops a broad pattern, theory, or generalization. Grounded theory is the classic example of this inductive use.

In mixed methods research, the use of theory depends on the specific [mixed methods design](../W5_Mixed_Methods/README.md). If the study is a sequential design, the theory might guide the quantitative phase deductively and then frame the qualitative follow-up. If the study has a transformative agenda, the theory serves as an overarching framework for both the quantitative and qualitative components. The researcher must explicitly state the theoretical framework early in the proposal to justify the integration of both types of data.

## Detailed Reading 2: Causal Mechanisms and Variables in Quantitative Research

In quantitative research, the use of theory is strictly deductive. The researcher starts with a theory, formulates specific hypotheses or research questions, collects data, and tests whether the data confirms the theory. This process requires the careful operationalization of variables. 

A variable is a characteristic or attribute of an individual or organization that can be measured or observed. Researchers classify variables based on their role in the theoretical model:
1. Independent variables (predictors): These variables influence or affect outcomes. In experimental studies, researchers manipulate these variables.
2. Dependent variables (outcomes): These variables represent the outcomes or results influenced by the independent variables.
3. Mediating variables: These variables stand between the independent and dependent variables. They transmit the effect of the independent variable to the dependent variable. [Mediation analysis](../W13_Mediation/README.md) requires specific statistical procedures.
4. Moderating variables: These variables affect the direction or strength of the relationship between the independent and dependent variables.
5. Confounding variables: These are unmeasured variables that influence the outcome but cannot be directly observed. Failing to account for confounding variables leads to spurious correlations.

To clarify relationships between variables, quantitative researchers construct theoretical visual models. These models use diagrams to connect variables with directional arrows. Researchers place independent variables on the left, dependent variables on the right, and mediating/moderating variables in the center. The arrows represent the hypothesized causal relationships, often marked with positive (+) or negative (-) signs to indicate the expected direction of the effect.

A central goal of quantitative research is establishing causality. However, statistical correlation between two variables does not prove a causal relationship. To prove causality, researchers must establish three conditions:
1. Temporal precedence: The cause must precede the effect in time.
2. Covariation: The cause and effect must be related.
3. Non-spuriousness: The researcher must rule out all plausible alternative explanations for the relationship.

Experimental designs, where the researcher randomly assigns participants to treatment and control groups, are the strongest method for isolating causal mechanisms. In observational studies (such as cross-sectional surveys), researchers use advanced statistical controls (like multiple regression or instrumental variables) to mitigate the effects of confounding variables. Regardless of the method, the causal claim must originate from a strong, established theory rather than purely from the data itself.
