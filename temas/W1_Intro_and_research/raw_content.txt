Large Language Models (LLM)
A Large Language Model (LLM) is a sophisticated mathematical function that predicts which word is likely to follow any given piece of text.
The LLM does not predict the exact word; instead, it assigns a probability to all the words that could follow in the text sequence.
Camarón que se duerme, se lo lleva la_______
In interaction with the user, the most probable words are presented based on the context of the “conversation”, but less probable words may also be included to give the text a more “natural” feel.

RLHF – Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback
To move beyond basic text completion, models undergo Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF).
Human annotators identify and flag unhelpful or problematic predictions. These corrections are incorporated into the model’s parameters.
Fine-tuning with RLHF encourages the model to generate responses that maximize reward signals, sometimes excessively.A well-known example involves an OpenAI model trained to favor positive sentiment: it concluded that weddings were the most positive topic and began generating wedding-related content regardless of the prompt.

This illustrates how the model may “play the training game”, optimizing for reward rather than coherence or relevance.

Constructivism (Interpretivism)
Frequently used in qualitative approaches.
It seeks to study perspectives, meanings, interpretations, and subjective experiences rather than reducing them to categories or predefined ideas.
1.
Understanding
Individuals construct the meaning of a situation within a specific temporal and spatial context.
2.
Multiple participants
Meanings are constructed socially and historically, shaped by social and cultural norms. Therefore, the processes through which individuals interact and integrate become highly significant.
3.
Theory generation
The researcher’s intention is to make sense of, or interpret, the meanings that others hold about the world.

Rather than beginning with a theory (as in post‑positivism), researchers generate or develop theories inductively, deriving patterns of meaning from participants’ accounts.

Positivism (the Scientific Method)
Positivism represents the traditional form of research, and its assumptions remain relevant for both quantitative and qualitative approaches.
1.
Determinism
It maintains a deterministic philosophy in which causes determine effects. There is a need to identify and assess the causes that influence outcomes.
2.
Reductionism
This perspective aims to reduce ideas to a small, discrete set to be tested, such as the variables that constitute hypotheses and research questions.
3.
Observation and measurement
It focuses on the careful observation and measurement of objective reality, using numerical measures of observations. The study of individual behavior becomes essential to this approach.
4.
Theory verification
Within the scientific method, research begins with a theory that is evaluated through data collection and evidence. Revisions are then made, followed by additional testing.

Transformative
Emerges as a response to the structural laws and theories of positivism, which often fail to reflect the reality of marginalised social groups affected by issues of power, social justice, discrimination, and oppression.
1.
Political
This worldview holds that research must be intertwined with politics and a political agenda aimed at confronting social oppression wherever it occurs.
2.
Oriented towards power and justice
Researchers must address important social issues such as empowerment, inequality, oppression, domination, suppression, and alienation.
3.
Change oriented
Research includes an action agenda for reform that can transform the lives of participants, the institutions in which they work or live, and the researcher’s own life.
4.
Collaborative
It assumes that the researcher will work collaboratively so as not to further marginalise participants. Participants may help design questions, collect data, analyse information, or share in the benefits of the research.

Quantitative
Non‑experimental quantitative research includes causal‑comparative research, in which researchers compare groups based on an independent variable that has already been observed.

It uses correlational statistics to describe and measure the relationships between variables. These designs may involve complex relationships between variables, employing techniques such as structural equation modelling, hierarchical linear modelling, and logistic regression.
Recent quantitative strategies have incorporated complex experiments with multiple variables and treatments, such as factorial designs and repeated‑measures designs.

These designs frequently rely on longitudinal data collection to identify how ideas and trends develop over time and often include sophisticated structural equation models that identify causal pathways.

Mixed
Mixed methods research involves the collection of both quantitative and qualitative data and the combination or integration of these data to generate insights or inferences. 

The joint use of quantitative and qualitative data helps neutralise the weaknesses of each type of data.

The combination of data sources therefore becomes a means of seeking convergence between qualitative and quantitative approaches.
Systematic integration of quantitative and qualitative data has led to the development of different ways of combining data across various kinds of research designs.

Research approaches
1.
Quantitative Approach: Post‑positivist Perspective
Design: Experimental design with pre‑test and post‑test measures of attitudes
The researcher tests a theory by specifying clear hypotheses and collecting data to support or refute them.

An experimental design is used to assess participants’ attitudes before and after an experimental treatment.

Data are gathered using an instrument that measures attitudes, and the results are analysed using statistical procedures and hypothesis testing.
2.
Qualitative Approach: Constructivist Perspective
Design: Ethnographic design with behavioural observation
The researcher seeks to understand the meaning of a phenomenon from the perspectives of participants.

This involves identifying a group that shares a common culture and studying how shared behavioural patterns develop over time.

A key component of this design is the direct observation of participants as they take part in relevant activities.
3.
Mixed Methods Approach: Pragmatic Perspective
Design: Sequential explanatory design
Mixed methods research assumes that collecting multiple types of data provides a more complete understanding of a research problem than relying solely on quantitative or qualitative evidence.

The study begins with a broad survey, allowing the researcher to generalise findings to a population.
In a second phase, open‑ended qualitative interviews are conducted to obtain detailed perspectives that help explain the results of the initial quantitative phase.

R Markdown
R Markdown provides an authoring framework for data science, allowing code to be saved and executed while generating high-quality reports from a single file.
It enables the reproducibility of results: both the code and the textual interpretation are in the same document.
Results are generated automatically from the source code.
It can be used as a library within R.
After installing the library, you can start a new document via the File menu. Markdown documents use a specific file type: .rmd.
Select File > New File > R Markdown (Steps 1 and 2).
Provide a title and a date (Steps 3 and 4).
Choose a format: HTML, PDF, or Word. In this course, we will mostly use PDF (which requires the tinytex package)
A template consists of three types of elements:
Metadata (Orange): Facilitates the construction and compilation of the document.
Code Chunks (Purple): Where commands, functions, and operations are written using the R language.
Text (Violet): Not conditioned by R language elements; mostly incorporates elements similar to HTML text construction.
To compile the document, use the Knit option (Step 5). You will then be prompted for a file name and directory to export the final PDF
