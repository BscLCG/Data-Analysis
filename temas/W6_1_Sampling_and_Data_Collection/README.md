## Detailed Reading: Sample Design and Instrumentation

Once a researcher selects a design, they must identify the population and extract a sample. A population consists of all individuals that meet the inclusion criteria for the study. Because surveying an entire population is rarely feasible, researchers select a sample.

### Sampling Strategies
Sampling strategies fall into two categories:
1. **Probability Sampling**: Every individual in the population has a known, non-zero probability of being selected. Techniques include simple random sampling, stratified sampling, and cluster sampling. This strategy allows researchers to make statistical inferences about the broader population.
2. **Non-Probability Sampling**: Selection is based on non-random criteria, such as convenience or specific purposive judgments. While useful in qualitative or exploratory designs, non-probability sampling prevents statistical generalization to the population.

### Statistical Power and Sample Size
In quantitative designs, determining the correct sample size requires a power analysis. Statistical power is the probability that a statistical test will correctly reject a false null hypothesis (i.e., detect an effect when one truly exists). A power analysis calculates the minimum required sample size based on three factors:
1. The **Alpha Level ($\alpha$)**: The probability of a Type I error (commonly set at 0.05).
2. The **Power Level ($1 - \beta$)**: The probability of avoiding a Type II error (commonly set at 0.80).
3. The **Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)**: The smallest true effect size the researcher expects to find.

Failing to conduct a power analysis risks designing a study that is statistically underpowered, rendering the results inconclusive.

### Survey Instrument Design
Survey instruments must translate theoretical constructs into measurable questions. Poor question design introduces measurement error. Researchers must avoid:
- **Leading questions** that suggest a preferred answer.
- **Double-barreled questions** that ask two separate things simultaneously.
- **Ambiguous terminology** that participants may interpret differently.

Digital survey platforms (like Qualtrics) allow for skip logic and branching, which ensure that participants only see questions relevant to their previous answers, reducing survey fatigue.

### Validity and Reliability
An instrument must be both valid and reliable. 
- **Reliability** refers to the consistency of the measure. If an instrument measures the same underlying construct multiple times under identical conditions, it should produce the same result. Internal consistency is often measured using Cronbach’s alpha.
- **Validity** refers to accuracy—whether the instrument actually measures what it claims to measure. 
  - *Construct validity*: The extent to which the items accurately capture the theoretical concept.
  - *Internal validity*: The extent to which the design eliminates confounding variables.
  - *External validity*: The extent to which the findings generalize across different populations, settings, and times.
