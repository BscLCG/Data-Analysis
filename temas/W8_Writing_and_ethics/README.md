# Week 8: Writing Strategies and Ethical Considerations

## Overview

This week focuses on the mechanics of writing a solid research proposal and the ethical guidelines that must be followed throughout the research lifecycle. It provides specific structural templates for quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods proposals, practical advice on paragraph construction and writing style, and a comprehensive overview of ethical standards to protect participants and ensure research integrity.

## Key Concepts

- **Proposal Structures:**
  - *Quantitative:* Introduction, Literature Review, Methods, Results, Discussion.
  - *Qualitative:* Introduction, Procedures (focusing on researcher role and narrative structure), Expected Results.
  - *Mixed Methods:* Incorporates both qualitative and quantitative structures along with explicit sections for integration and joint displays.
- **Writing Mechanics:**
  - *Paragraphs:* Basic building blocks consisting of a topic sentence, body sentences, evidence sentences, and a closing sentence.
  - *Style:* Emphasis on clear, concise writing, avoiding the passive voice, and establishing a regular writing habit.
- **Ethical Considerations:**
  - *Before the study:* IRB approval, site permissions, and protocol design.
  - *During data collection:* Informed consent, avoiding deception, and treating participants equitably.
  - *Data analysis and reporting:* Protecting anonymity, reporting contradictory findings, avoiding plagiarism, and sharing data responsibly.

## Relevant Points

- **The Importance of Structure:** Each paragraph should represent a single unit of thought. Topic sentences should introduce new ideas, while closing sentences should consolidate the argument.
- **Active Voice:** Writing in the active voice makes research reports more direct and engaging compared to the passive voice.
- **Ethical Vigilance:** Ethics go beyond just obtaining consent; they include how data is analyzed, how marginalized groups are treated, and how findings are eventually reported without falsification or bias.

## Detailed Reading 1: Structuring and Writing Research Proposals

Writing a research proposal requires a clear structure. The structure varies depending on the research design. Regardless of the design, the proposal must present a coherent picture of the entire project, moving logically from problem formulation to methodology.

A quantitative proposal follows a standard sequential model. It begins with an introduction that states the problem, followed directly by an extensive [literature review](../W3_Literature_review_and_Logistic/README.md) to justify the variables. The next section details the methods, including population, sampling procedures, and the specific statistical models. Finally, the proposal outlines expected results and discussion points.

A qualitative proposal operates differently. Because qualitative research explores emergent themes, there is no single dominant structure. The proposal begins with an introduction stating the problem but relies less on a rigid literature review. Instead, the procedures section focuses heavily on the researcher's role, philosophical assumptions (like the constructivist worldview), and the data collection processes (such as interviews or observations). It also outlines how the final narrative will be structured.

A mixed methods proposal combines elements of both. It begins by stating a problem that specifically requires both types of data. It poses separate quantitative and qualitative research questions, followed by a specific mixed methods integration question. The methods section explicitly defines the chosen [mixed methods design](../W5_Mixed_Methods/README.md) (convergent, sequential) and presents a diagram of the integration process. 

Once the structure is set, the researcher must write clearly. In academic writing, paragraphs are the basic building blocks. Each paragraph should contain a single unit of thought. A standard academic paragraph consists of four parts:
1. Topic sentence: Introduces the new focus of attention. It signals a change in topic and should never look backward.
2. Body sentences: Present the central argument, explain reasoning, or describe results.
3. Evidence sentences: Provide facts, citations, or examples to support the body sentences.
4. Closing sentence: Pulls the argument together and creates a transition to the next paragraph.

Researchers must also use clear language. Writing in the active voice ("The researchers identified barriers") is direct and preferred over the passive voice ("Barriers were identified by the researchers"). Researchers establish a writing habit by working in short, daily blocks rather than intermittent marathons. Consistent writing maintains the workflow and conceptual continuity.

## Detailed Reading 2: Ethical Considerations in Research

Scientific research demands strict ethical compliance at every stage. Anticipating potential ethical issues protects human participants and ensures the integrity of the data. Ethics go beyond simply avoiding physical harm; they involve justice, privacy, transparency, and accurate reporting.

Before conducting the study, researchers must obtain approval from an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or equivalent ethics committee. They must consult the specific ethical codes of their discipline and secure local permissions from the research sites. A core requirement is minimizing the burden placed on participants. The researcher must design efficient [data collection instruments](../W6_Data_collection/README.md) and respect participants' time.

At the beginning of the study, the researcher must clearly disclose the study's purpose. Participants must sign informed consent forms without pressure or coercion. The researcher must inform participants that they have the right to refuse participation or withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. Special precautions must be taken for vulnerable populations, such as children or marginalized groups, ensuring that the research respects their social norms and specific needs.

During data collection, the researcher must treat all participants equally. Deception is unacceptable unless strictly justified by the experimental design and followed by immediate debriefing. The researcher must adhere strictly to the established data collection protocols. They must avoid biased questions, refrain from expressing personal opinions, and ensure that their presence causes minimal disruption to the research site. 

During data analysis and reporting, the researcher must maintain absolute objectivity. The researcher must report all findings, including contradictory results or data that fails to support the hypothesis. Selecting only positive results constitutes academic fraud. Privacy is paramount; researchers must assign fictitious names or aliases to protect participants' identities and store all raw data securely.

Finally, researchers must avoid all forms of plagiarism and properly credit intellectual ownership to co-authors, assistants, and participants. They must disclose all funding sources and potential conflicts of interest. The ethical framework follows the "NUDGE FORGOOD" acronym, ensuring Fairness, Openness, Respect, Goals, Options, Opinions, and Delegation are prioritized throughout the research lifecycle.
