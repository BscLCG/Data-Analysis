# Week 14: Final Project Presentations

## Overview

This final week is dedicated to the students' data analysis project presentations. Students apply the methodologies, theories, and techniques (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-methods) learned throughout the course to answer specific, real-world research questions.

## Key Concepts

- **Applied Research:** Transitioning from theory and methodology to practical execution and communication of results.
- **Presentation Skills:** Synthesizing literature reviews, methodologies, and findings into a concise 25-minute presentation.
- **Peer Review and Feedback:** A dedicated Q&A session to provide and receive critical observations.

## Relevant Points

- **Diverse Topics:** Presentations cover a wide range of social, economic, and psychological issues, including:
  - *Political Science:* Analyzing electoral abstention for Youth Councils among university students in Bogota.
  - *Occupational Health:* Examining occupational stress among university professors through the lens of course types and research responsibilities.
  - *Mixed-Methods Analysis:* Modeling childbearing intentions using feminist identity frameworks and the Miller TDIB model.

## Detailed Reading: Presenting Applied Data Analysis

The culmination of the research process is the communication of findings. A study holds no value if its methodology, results, and implications are not clearly transmitted to an audience. In the final week of the course, researchers transition from data processing to public presentation. 

Presenting applied data analysis requires synthesizing complex, multi-month projects into strict timeframes. In this environment, each research group has 25 minutes to present their complete study, followed by 10 minutes of questions and observations from peers and faculty. This format mimics academic conferences and professional consulting environments. 

The structure of the presentation mirrors the structure of a [formal research proposal](../W8_Writing_and_ethics/README.md). However, instead of proposing future work, the presenters must deliver concrete findings. 

A successful presentation begins with the research problem and the primary research question. The presenters must justify the relevance of the problem using key literature. Following the theoretical framing, the presentation shifts to the methodology. For quantitative projects, this involves defining the target population, the [sampling strategy](../W6_Data_collection/README.md), the operationalization of variables, and the specific statistical model used (e.g., [Logistic regression](../W3_Literature_review_and_Logistic/README.md)). For qualitative or [mixed-methods projects](../W5_Mixed_Methods/README.md), the methodology section must detail the integration process, the collection of open data, and the analytical framework.

The core of the presentation focuses on the results. Researchers use visual aids—such as tables, [theoretical visual models](../W4_Use_of_theory/README.md), and statistical plots generated during [Exploratory Data Analysis](../W2_EDA_and_research_questions/README.md)—to convey complex relationships quickly. The presentation concludes with a discussion of the implications of the findings and the specific limitations of the study's design.

The projects presented in this course demonstrate the diversity of applied data analysis in the social sciences. Three notable examples illustrate this scope:

1. Electoral Abstention: A political science project analyzing the mechanisms driving low voter turnout among university students for Youth Councils in Bogota. The study investigates why a highly educated demographic with significant access to information systematically disengages from specific electoral processes.

2. Occupational Stress: A health and labor study examining the occupational stress levels of university professors in the Finance, Government, and International Relations faculty. The research utilizes [mediation models](../W13_Mediation/README.md) to test whether specific course types (e.g., quantitative vs. theoretical), research load, and problem-solving pedagogy mediate the relationship between a professor's program affiliation and their stress levels.

3. Childbearing Intentions: A mixed-methods study exploring the motivations underlying the childbearing intentions of undergraduate students. The research employs an explanatory sequential design, leveraging feminist identity theory and Miller's Traits-Desires-Intentions-Behavior (TDIB) model to map how ideological frameworks shape demographic decisions.

The peer review session following each presentation is a critical component of the scientific method. Reviewers evaluate the internal and external validity of the study, the appropriateness of the statistical models, and the clarity of the theoretical constructs. Receiving and addressing this critical feedback ensures the robustness of the final research output.
