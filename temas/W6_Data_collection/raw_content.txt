The Qualtrics Environment
When you log in to Qualtrics, the first page you will see is the control panel. Here you can create and manage your projects. A project is built from a predefined survey template or from scratch (a new survey-built step by step by you). Within the control panel, projects are organized into folders on the left side of the page.
To create a new survey, go to the "Create Project" menu, then select creating a survey from scratch, and click the "Get started" button in the panel that appears on the right side of the page.
Assign a short name to your project and select "Start with a blank survey" from the templates menu. In addition to creating a blank project, Qualtrics offers three additional options: "Import a QSF File": lets you upload a survey in Qualtrics Survey Format (QSF). "Copy a Survey from an Existing Project": lets you duplicate one of your existing surveys and use it as a base for the new one. "Use a Survey from Your Library": lets you use existing surveys available in the Qualtrics Library.
After this, the project will appear on your control panel. Once the project is created, you can reorganize folders and subfolders to, for example, separate instruments with a qualitative versus quantitative focus. When you open one of your projects from the control panel, you will find the instrument development area. This page has 5 main tabs:
In the Survey tab, you can edit the survey you have opened.
In the Workflows tab, you can configure different types of notifications that will be triggered if a participant responds in a particular way (for example, selects a specific response option).
In the Distributions tab, you can activate your survey and also have different options for how to distribute it. This is especially useful if you are conducting your research online.
In the Data & Analysis tab, you can view individual responses or averages from your survey. However, to truly test your hypothesis, you will need to download the data and analyze it in Stata, R, SPSS, or another statistical software.
In the Reports tab, you can create different visual representations of your survey results.
Within the Survey tab, you will find 6 tools to customize and optimize the questions in your instrument:
Question Type: allows you to select the format or type of question to include in the survey. The available options are:
Multiple Choice: can be displayed as a traditional list of options or as a dropdown, selection box, etc.
Text Entry: used to collect open-ended responses from respondents. These responses can be long essays or short text.
Text/Graphic: can be used to add an introduction or instructions to your survey, or to display an asset such as an image.
Matrix: used to collect multiple pieces of information in one question. This type provides an efficient way to condense your survey or group similar items in a single question.
Slider: sliders allow respondents to indicate their level of preference with a draggable bar instead of a traditional button or checkbox.
Form Field: allows collecting standard form information such as name and email address.
Rank Order/Preference: used to determine each respondent's order of preference for a list of items.
Side by Side: allows asking multiple questions in a condensed table, providing an efficient way to shorten your survey while collecting the same amount of data.
Response Type: defines whether respondents can select a single or multiple answer options. This allows adjusting response flexibility according to the instrument's needs.
Choices: here you configure the different response options available to respondents. You can add, edit, or delete options. You can also use suggested options or edit multiple options at once.
Format: determines how response options will be displayed, such as in a vertical list, horizontal, or dropdown format, depending on what is most appropriate for the form.
Response Requirements: allows setting rules or validations for responses, such as making the question mandatory or setting specific criteria that responses must meet.
Question Behavior: offers advanced settings such as adding display logic (show or hide the question under certain conditions), skip logic (skip questions based on responses), randomization of options, and value recoding.
Creating Question Blocks
In Qualtrics, a question block is a set of (different types of) questions grouped within the same section of a survey. Blocks allow you to organize and structure surveys more efficiently, facilitating their management and modification. Each block can contain questions related to a specific topic or serve a particular function within the survey.
The advantage of organizing different parts of a survey into blocks is that specific functions available in Qualtrics can be applied to all the questions within the same block. One of the main advantages of this organization is that it allows, for example, randomly assigning 50% of participants to one form while the other 50% are assigned to another.
Example Questionnaire with Random Block Assignment
Following the instrument developed by Professor Dario Krpan, we will generate an instrument to investigate whether people's self-perception of time management can be influenced using default values, even when they did not receive an intervention that objectively changed their time management.
More specifically, we will create a survey that will allow us to test whether setting participants' default answers in the time management questionnaire to "never" or "none" influences them to report less effective time management compared to setting these default responses to "very often" or "many".
Therefore, our goal will be to build a simple survey consisting of 5 time management items taken from Oettingen et al. (2015). All participants in this experiment will respond to these 5 items. However, for half of the participants (Condition 0), the default response will be "never" or "none", while for the other half it will be "very often" or "many" (Condition 1). Each participant will be randomly assigned to one of the two experimental conditions. Applying this instrument will allow us to study the following hypothesis:
For this purpose, the first block of the form will contain the informed consent form that participants must read to confirm their willingness to participate in the experiment.
The second and third blocks will include the five items of the time management questionnaire. In the second block, the default options will be set to "never" or "none" (Condition 0), while in the third block the default options will be set to "very often" or "many" (Condition 1). The organization of the instrument must allow randomly assigning 50% of participants to Condition 0, while the other 50% will be assigned to Condition 1.

Block Randomization
We have created 2 Condition Blocks (0 and 1), but participants in our experiment will be randomly assigned to only one of them.
For this you must go to the Survey Flow section. Here you can organize and control the structure of the survey. In this section, you can manage content blocks, such as questions or experimental conditions, through options to move, duplicate, or delete elements, and you can add new functionalities such as "Randomizer" to randomly assign participants to different conditions.
To use this feature, first click on the "Add a New Element Here" menu and select "Randomizer". The randomizer must be moved below the "Informed Consent" block in the survey flow. To do this, press "Move" and hold down the left mouse button (it is very important that you keep the mouse button held down, otherwise you will not be able to move the randomizer). The field will turn green, and you must drag it below the "Informed Consent" block.
Now it is necessary to convert the "Condition 0" and "Condition 1" blocks into "Randomizer" elements. To do this, first press "Move" within the "Condition 0" block (similar to how it was done with the randomizer in the previous example) and then drag it to "Add a New Element Here". Do the same with "Condition 1".
Finally, select "Randomly present 1 of the following elements" within the "Randomizer". Also, check the "Evenly Present Elements" option. These features will ensure that only 1 condition is randomly shown to each participant and that a similar number of participants complete each of the two conditions. When finished, click the "Apply" button in the lower right corner of the page.
Creating a Variable to Store Condition Assignment
When a participant completes your form and you download the data, the dataset will not by default include a variable indicating which condition the participant was assigned to. However, this variable is important, as it constitutes the "independent variable" you will need for your statistical analyses. Therefore, it is necessary to create a variable using the "Embedded Data" functionality, which allows recording additional information beyond the responses to the questions.
First, in the "Survey Flow" tab in the left menu, click on "Add a New Element Here" and select "Branch". Create 2 branches, one for each condition of the form.
Now that the 2 "Branch" elements have been created, you will need to click on "Add a Condition" within each branch to determine the conditions under which they should be triggered. For the first branch, select the following logic:
"Then, trigger the branch if question C0-1 (the first question in Condition 0) and Choice 1 (or simply '1', or sometimes '1 ', depending on the operating system and browser) and is displayed."
For the second branch, the logic to select is: "Then, trigger the branch if question C1-1 (the first question in Condition 1) and Choice 1 (or simply '1', or sometimes '1 ', depending on the operating system and browser) and is displayed."
Click "OK" in the lower right corner of each branch to save the logic. These rules mean that the first branch will be triggered whenever participants are assigned to Condition 0 (because the first question in Condition 0 is shown when that condition is active) and the second branch will be triggered whenever participants are assigned to Condition 1 (by the same logic with Condition 1).
Now we need to create 2 "Embedded Data" elements to record that a participant is in Condition 0 when the first branch is triggered, and that a participant is in Condition 1 when the second branch is triggered. To create the 2 "Embedded Data" elements, click on "Add a New Element Here" twice, and then, for each element, select "Embedded Data".
After this, you will need to move the first "Embedded Data" element inside the first "Branch" and the second "Embedded Data" element inside the second "Branch". To do this, press "Move" within each "Embedded Data" element and drag it to "Add a New Element Here" under the corresponding branch.
The next step is to "Set Embedded Data". To do this, in each "Embedded Data" element, click on "Create new field or choose from dropdown", and type "Condition" in the text box. Then click "Set a value now" and enter 0 for the first "Embedded Data" element and 1 for the second "Embedded Data" element.
Finally, when done, click the "Apply" button hidden in the lower right corner of the page. Now, these embedded data elements will create a variable called "Condition", where 0 will be assigned to all participants assigned to Condition 0 and 1 to participants assigned to Condition 1. This variable will appear in your dataset when you download it.
Form Preview
Before activating the survey and sending it to participants, it is important to test it ourselves to make sure it looks good and does exactly what we want it to do. To test the survey, we will use the preview function. Press "Preview" and then answer the questions yourself to confirm that everything works correctly.
Before showing how to launch and distribute the survey, it is important to review the "IQ Score". This tool evaluates aspects such as survey length, whether it is suitable for mobile devices, the number of grammatical conjunctions in question text, among others. It then calculates how "smart" the survey is overall.

The law of large numbers tells us that, for large samples, the sample mean is very likely to be very similar to the population mean.
In other words, if we were to draw infinitely many random samples and compute the sample mean Avg[Y] for each of them, the average of all these sample means would converge to the true population mean, E[Y].
A related property states that the expected value of the sample mean, Avg[Y], is equal to the population expected value, E[Y].

What statistical tests should be used?
A statistical test is a rule defined a priori (i.e., before accessing the data) that specifies what must be observed in the data to justify rejecting the null hypothesis (H₀).

A result meeting this criterion is labelled “statistically significant.”
What is required for a statistical test?
Technically, the significance level may be set at any value, provided it is chosen before examining the data.

The general procedure for hypothesis testing consists of setting the significance level α at a small value (often 0.05) so that the probability of committing a Type I error is limited.
Given a fixed value of α, we select a statistical test, defined by its rejection region, that makes β (the probability of a Type II error) as small as possible.

In other words, we aim to find the most powerful test for the specified significance level.

Quantitative designs
Power of the test (1 − β)
Power is the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when the true effect is not zero (e.g., ATE ≠ 0 or the correlation is non‑zero). 

A conventional benchmark is 80% power: β is the Type II error, failing to reject H₀ when it is false (a false negative).

An experimental design is said to have 80% statistical power if 80% of all possible random assignments would produce observable results (i.e., allow detection of the effect) when the effect actually exists.

Dado que el valor p es 0.065, el mayor nivel de significancia al que podemos realizar esta prueba y no rechazarla es el 6,5%.

Si realizamos la prueba a un nivel inferior al 6,5% (por ejemplo, al 5%), no rechazaremos H0.
 Si realizamos la prueba a un nivel superior al 6,5% (por ejemplo, al 10%), rechazamos H0. 

El valor p en este ejemplo tiene otra interpretación útil: es la probabilidad de que observemos un valor de T tan grande como 1.52 cuando la hipótesis nula es cierta. 

Si la hipótesis nula es realmente cierta, observaríamos un valor de T tan grande como 1.52 debido al azar sólo el 6.5% de las veces. Que esto sea lo suficientemente pequeño como para rechazar H0 depende de nuestra tolerancia a un error de tipo I.

Diseños cuantitativos
Si  se quiere estimar el valor medio de una variable relevante en una población, se pueden aplicar métodos de muestreo.

 Debido a la naturaleza estocástica del proceso de selección de la muestra, algunas observaciones estarán por encima o por debajo del promedio general.

Por lo tanto, no todas las estimaciones de la muestra serán exactamente iguales al promedio verdadero de la población.

Estas diferencias con respecto a la media de la población son los errores.
 Con suficientes observaciones de la muestra, este error tiende a cero, esperamos que los errores sean aleatorios, no sistemáticos.
