# Week 5: Mixed Methods Procedures

## Overview

This week introduces the rationale and procedures for conducting Mixed Methods research. It details how the integration of quantitative (closed) and qualitative (open) data can provide a deeper, more robust understanding of a research problem than either method alone. The materials cover basic mixed methods designs, complex configurations, and the specific procedures for integrating and visualizing different types of data.

## Key Concepts

- **Mixed Methods Characteristics:** The collection, analysis, and integration of both quantitative and qualitative data within a single study or series of studies.
- **Data Types:**
  - *Open Data (Qualitative):* Information where predefined response options are not provided (e.g., interviews, observations).
  - *Closed Data (Quantitative):* Predefined response options (e.g., rating scales, test scores).
- **Integration:** The process of merging or connecting the two databases.
- **Joint Displays:** Tables or charts that present the side-by-side combination of the two data sources to facilitate comparison.
- **Meta-inferences:** Conclusions that emerge specifically from the integration of the two methods.
- **Basic Designs:**
  - *Convergent:* Merges both types of data concurrently to compare results and find convergence or divergence.
  - *Explanatory Sequential:* Collects quantitative data first, followed by qualitative data to help explain the initial statistical results.
  - *Exploratory Sequential:* Collects qualitative data first to explore a phenomenon, using the findings to build a quantitative instrument for broader testing.
- **Complex Designs:** Embedding mixed methods within larger frameworks such as experiments, case studies, evaluation processes, or participatory action research.

## Relevant Points

- **Added Value:** Mixing methods neutralizes the limitations of a single approach and leverages their respective strengths.
- **Sampling Strategy:** In sequential designs, the qualitative sample is often a subset of the quantitative sample (Explanatory), whereas in exploratory designs, the samples are drawn from the same population but typically consist of different individuals.
- **Design Selection:** The choice of design is dictated by the study's purpose—whether the researcher aims to compare findings, explain surprising statistical results, or develop a culturally specific measurement instrument.

## Detailed Reading 1: The Design Foundations of Mixed Methods

Mixed methods research is not simply having two datasets in the same study; it requires deliberate integration. Researchers use this approach when a single methodology cannot fully address the research problem. For instance, quantitative data captures magnitude and frequency across a large population, but lacks context. Qualitative data captures deep, contextual mechanisms from a few individuals, but cannot generalize. Mixed methods neutralize these respective weaknesses.

The core foundation relies on combining "closed" and "open" data. Closed data refers to quantitative information where the researcher predetermines the response options, such as Likert scales on a survey. Open data refers to qualitative information where participants construct the responses in their own words, such as transcripts from semi-structured interviews. 

To design a mixed methods study, the researcher must align it with the [pragmatic worldview](../W1_Intro_and_research/README.md). Pragmatism dictates that the research problem is more important than strict methodological purity. The researcher focuses on the consequences and applications of the findings, merging whatever tools are necessary to answer the questions.

Researchers also use complex designs by embedding mixed methods within larger frameworks. For example, in an experimental study testing a new pedagogical tool, the researcher collects quantitative test scores to evaluate effectiveness. Simultaneously, the researcher collects qualitative interview data to understand how the participants experienced the tool. This embedding process happens in case studies, participatory action research, and policy evaluation. 

## Detailed Reading 2: Methodological Implementation and Basic Designs

Implementing a mixed methods study requires choosing one of three basic designs: Convergent, Explanatory Sequential, or Exploratory Sequential.

1. Convergent Design: The researcher collects both quantitative and qualitative data at the same time. The goal is to compare the two databases to see if they converge or diverge. The researcher analyzes the data sets separately and then merges the results. If the survey data shows high job satisfaction but the interview data reveals deep frustration, the researcher must investigate this divergence.

2. Explanatory Sequential Design: The researcher conducts the study in two distinct phases. Phase one is strictly quantitative. The researcher collects data, runs statistical models (like [logistic regression](../W3_Literature_review_and_Logistic/README.md)), and identifies significant, surprising, or extreme results. Phase two is qualitative and specifically targets these results. For example, if a model shows a statistically significant drop in performance for a specific demographic, the researcher conducts interviews with individuals from that demographic to explain the statistical mechanism. The qualitative data explains the quantitative results.

3. Exploratory Sequential Design: The researcher reverses the order. Phase one is qualitative. The researcher explores a topic without predetermined variables, gathering themes and quotes. Phase two uses those findings to build a quantitative instrument, such as a new survey. This design is necessary when existing instruments are culturally inappropriate or when the variables are entirely unknown.

A critical methodological challenge is sampling. In a convergent design, researchers use the same or parallel samples. In an explanatory sequential design, the qualitative sample must be a direct subset of the quantitative sample; otherwise, the interviews cannot validly explain the survey data. In an exploratory design, the researcher uses different individuals from the same population for both phases to prevent the qualitative participants from skewing the subsequent quantitative test.

Integration is the final methodological step. Researchers cannot present the quantitative results and qualitative results in isolated chapters. They must merge them into meta-inferences. To achieve this, researchers use joint displays. A joint display is a table or matrix that maps the statistical results directly alongside the qualitative quotes or themes. Another integration technique is data transformation, where researchers quantify qualitative data by counting the frequency of a theme and running statistical tests on those counts.
